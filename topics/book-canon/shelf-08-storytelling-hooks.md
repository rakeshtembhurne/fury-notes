# Storytelling Craft + Hooks + Video — 25-Book Canon
Audience: technical founder learning selling; shy on camera, needs story structure + hooks.
All titles verified against Open Library (Sep 2026). Format: thesis + 3 actionable tactics.

## A. Story Structure (classics)

### 1. The Hero with a Thousand Faces — Joseph Campbell (1949)
Thesis: All myths share one monomyth arc — a hero leaves the ordinary world, faces trials, and returns transformed.
- Map your founder/customer story onto departure → initiation → return; name the "ordinary world" pain first.
- Cast the customer as hero and your product as the mentor/guide, never the hero.
- Use the "return with the elixir" beat: every story ends with the concrete transformation, not a feature list.

### 2. Story — Robert McKee (1997)
Thesis: Story works through value change driven by conflict; every scene must turn a value from positive to negative or back.
- Build each pitch/video scene around one value flip (e.g., confidence → doubt → confidence); cut scenes with no turn.
- Define your controlling idea (value + cause) in one sentence before writing anything.
- Use the gap between expectation and result to create the surprise that holds attention.

### 3. The Writer's Journey — Christopher Vogler (1992; 3rd ed. 2007)
Thesis: Campbell's monomyth compressed into 12 practical stages + 7 archetypes usable for any narrative, including business.
- Structure launch/demo videos on the 12 stages; treat "refusal of the call" as the objection-handling beat.
- Assign archetypes explicitly: you = mentor, skeptic customer = threshold guardian, testimonial = ally.
- Open in the ordinary world for ≤10% of runtime, then cross the threshold (the hook/problem) fast.

### 4. The Anatomy of Story — John Truby (2007)
Thesis: Great stories are built from 22 organic steps driven by a hero's moral weakness and need, not formulaic 3-act beats.
- Give every story a ghost/weakness (the flawed belief your customer starts with) and a need (what they must learn).
- Build a four-corner opposition: hero, opponent, ally, fake-ally opponent — map competitors and skeptics onto them.
- Write the moral self-revelation first (the lesson), then reverse-engineer the plot that proves it.

### 5. Save the Cat! — Blake Snyder (2005)
Thesis: Commercial stories follow a 15-beat sheet (opening image → finale) that audiences unconsciously expect.
- Beat-sheet your 60–90s Reels: opening image (0–3s hook) → catalyst → break into act two → finale payoff.
- Put a literal "save the cat" moment early: one beat that makes you/the protagonist instantly likeable.
- Logline-test every video idea: irony + compelling mental image + audience + cost, in one sentence.

## B. Business / Brand Storytelling

### 6. Building a StoryBrand — Donald Miller (2017)
Thesis: Customers don't buy the best product; they buy the clearest story, told in the 7-part SB7 framework with the customer as hero.
- Run every landing page/demo through SB7: character → problem → guide → plan → call to action → stakes → success.
- Name the villain (even an abstract one like "chaos" or "wasted ad spend") — story needs an enemy.
- Give a 3-step plan + direct CTA; never end content without stating exactly what to do next.

### 7. Storyworthy — Matthew Dicks (2018)
Thesis: Memorable stories come from small 5-second moments ("Homework for Life") told with stakes, specificity, and the 5-4-3-2-1 arc.
- Do daily Homework for Life: write one story-worthy moment per day so you never run out of content.
- Start in the action (no throat-clearing backstory); add backstory only when the listener demands it.
- End on transformation, not summary: state how you changed, in one line — that's the takeaway.

### 8. Wired for Story — Lisa Cron (2012)
Thesis: The brain is neurologically wired to track stories as simulations for "what will this cost me / what do I gain" — story = cause-and-effect with stakes.
- Open with a misbelief + desire: what does the protagonist wrongly believe that creates the tension?
- Make every fact ride inside a cause-and-effect chain; brains drop unlinked information.
- Front-load stakes (what's at risk if nothing changes) before any explanation or data.

### 9. Storynomics — Robert McKee & Tom Gerace (2018)
Thesis: Story-driven marketing (storynomics) replaces interruptive ads: brands must become storytellers that earn attention.
- Replace one ad with one story-formed content piece per funnel stage (setup → complication → payoff).
- Measure story effectiveness by demand created (search, signups), not impressions.
- Audit content: if removing the brand logo leaves nothing interesting, it's an ad, not a story — rewrite it.

### 10. The Storyteller's Secret — Carmine Gallo (2016)
Thesis: The world's best communicators win by telling stories built on passion, heroes, and memorable simplicity (analyzed across 200+ talks).
- Collect 3 signature stories (origin, customer transformation, vision) and reuse them everywhere.
- Translate numbers into human-scale analogies; never quote a stat without a story around it.
- Practice the 3-act business story: setup (status quo pain) → confrontation (struggle) → resolution (your lesson).

## C. Stickiness, Virality & Hooks

### 11. Made to Stick — Chip Heath & Dan Heath (2007)
Thesis: Sticky ideas follow SUCCESs: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories.
- Kill the curse of knowledge: pre-test every explanation on a novice; whatever confuses them gets cut.
- Open loops with unexpected gaps ("the most important 3 seconds of your pitch are…") then close them.
- Swap abstractions for concrete sensory detail + one credible testimony (testable credential or vivid detail).

### 12. Contagious — Jonah Berger (2013)
Thesis: Virality is engineered via STEPPS: Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories.
- Engineer triggers: tie your message to everyday cues (Monday standup, chai break) so environments remind people of you.
- Wrap the message in high-arousal emotion (awe, amusement, righteous anger) — low-arousal content never spreads.
- Build sharing into the action itself (public visible use, referral mechanics), not as an afterthought.

### 13. Hook Point — Brendan Kane (2020)
Thesis: In a 3-second attention economy you must test and compress your message into a "hook point" that earns the next 3 seconds.
- Write 10 hook variants per video; test the first 3 seconds as standalone units before producing the rest.
- Lead with the payoff or the contrarian claim, then justify — never build up to the point.
- Study retention graphs like lab data: the exact frame where viewers drop is your hook failure point; reshoot from there.

### 14. Captivology — Ben Parr (2015)
Thesis: Attention follows 7 psychological captivation triggers (automaticity, framing, disruption, reward, reputation, mystery, acknowledgement).
- Use mystery gaps deliberately: promise an answer, delay it with escalating clues, then deliver.
- Frame with relevance ("if you run demos, this 30-second fix…") — attention follows self-interest.
- Acknowledge the audience (reply, name, feature comments) to convert passive viewers into loyal returners.

## D. YouTube & Short-Form Video

### 15. YouTube Secrets — Sean Cannell & Benji Travis (2018)
Thesis: Growth comes from a repeatable system: niche down, package titles/thumbnails for click + retention, publish consistently.
- Design title + thumbnail as one unit promising a single outcome; A/B test thumbnails on every upload.
- Script the first 15 seconds word-for-word (hook → credibility → roadmap) and improvise the rest.
- Commit to a series format (same day, same promise) so subscribers know exactly what they get.

### 16. The YouTube Formula — Derral Eves (2021)
Thesis: The algorithm rewards satisfaction (retention + engagement per impression); engineer videos around audience-driven ideation.
- Ideate from audience data (search, comments, outliers in niche), not from what you feel like making.
- Structure for retention: open loop in first 30s, re-hook every 60–90s, cut anything that doesn't serve the promise.
- Optimize for click-through × average view duration as the two numbers that matter; iterate packaging before content.

### 17. How to Shoot Video That Doesn't Suck — Steve Stockman (2011)
Thesis: Watchable video comes from a few fundamentals — think in shots, hold steady, get close, light faces, capture clean audio.
- Audio first: use a close mic (lavalier) — viewers forgive bad video, never bad audio.
- Shoot tight close-ups of faces for emotion; wide shots only to establish context, briefly.
- Hold each shot ≥10 seconds steady, move the camera only with a reason, and always shoot 3× more b-roll than you think.

## E. Rhetoric & Persuasion

### 18. Thank You for Arguing — Jay Heinrichs (2007; 3rd ed. 2017)
Thesis: Aristotle's rhetoric (ethos, pathos, logos + kairos) is a usable everyday operating system for winning arguments and trust.
- Open hard conversations with ethos: establish shared values and conceding a small point before your claim.
- Match the appeal to the moment: character doubts → ethos, emotional resistance → pathos, intellectual objection → logos.
- Use kairos (timing): make the ask when the audience is most receptive — right after a shared laugh or agreement.

### 19. The Elements of Eloquence — Mark Forsyth (2013)
Thesis: Memorable sentences are built from classical rhetorical figures (alliteration, tricolon, chiasmus…) anyone can learn.
- End key lines with a tricolon (three beats) — "we build, we break, we ship" — rhythm makes claims stick.
- Use chiasmus (ABBA reversal: "ask not what your tool can do for you…") for quotable one-liners.
- Deploy alliteration sparingly on the one phrase per talk you want repeated.

## F. Presenting & On-Camera Confidence

### 20. Resonate — Nancy Duarte (2010)
Thesis: Great presentations alternate "what is" vs. "what could be" in a three-act persuasive story ending in a call to a new bliss.
- Structure every deck as contrast pairs: current pain vs. possible future, alternating until the call to action.
- Start with the audience's world ("what is"), not your company — earn the right to present your idea.
- End with the transformed world + one concrete action; never end on Q&A or a thank-you slide.

### 21. Talk Like TED — Carmine Gallo (2014)
Thesis: Analysis of 500+ TED talks distills 9 secrets: emotional hooks, novelty, vivid delivery, and relentless rehearsal.
- Unleash the master storyteller: open with a personal anecdote that carries the talk's emotional core.
- Teach one jaw-dropping new fact ("wow" moment) per 10 minutes to trigger dopamine and recall.
- Rehearse aloud 10× minimum and record yourself — delivery (voice, gesture) carries ~2× the weight of words for persuasion.

### 22. TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public Speaking — Chris Anderson (2016)
Thesis: A talk is the transfer of one powerful idea via throughline, connection, and explanation techniques that respect the audience.
- Reduce every talk to one throughline sentence; cut anything that doesn't serve it.
- Build connection early: make eye contact, show vulnerability, and give the audience a reason to care in 60 seconds.
- Explain by dose: one concept at a time, each with example → implication → curiosity for the next.

### 23. Presentation Zen — Garr Reynolds (2008; 2nd ed. 2012)
Thesis: Slides should beZen-simple visuals that amplify the speaker; design with restraint, empty space, and image-driven storytelling.
- One idea per slide, full-bleed image + ≤6 words; move details to handouts, not slides.
- Use empty space aggressively — remove one element from every slide until removing more hurts meaning.
- Prepare analog (whiteboard the story arc first), then go digital; never start in slide software.

### 24. Steal the Show — Michael Port (2015)
Thesis: Performing (on stage or camera) is a trainable craft: mindset + rehearsal + audience-centric mechanics beat talent.
- Adopt the performer mindset: the performance is for the audience's transformation, not your validation — kills stage fright.
- Rehearse in performance conditions (standing, timed, recorded); do a full tech check before any recording.
- Open with high status energy (posture, breath, first line memorized cold) — audiences decide in 7 seconds.

### 25. Confessions of a Public Speaker — Scott Berkun (2009)
Thesis: Honest field notes demystifying speaking: fear is normal, attention is fragile, and craft (prep, feedback, recovery) wins.
- Fight fear with exposure + prep: arrive early, own the room, and have a memorized first 60 seconds as a safety net.
- Never apologize, never read slides, never go over time — the three fastest ways to lose a room.
- Solicit brutal feedback after every talk (one thing to keep, one to fix) and iterate like shipping software.
