# The 30 Greatest Books on Psychology, Persuasion, Influence, Decision-Making & Behavioral Economics

*Canon for a technical founder: bestsellers, academic cornerstones, and founder-recommended reading (Munger, Buffett, Gates, Thiel, startup canon).*
*Note: web-search verification was unavailable at build time (search backend returned no results); author/year entries below are canonical bibliographic data — spot-check any citation before publishing.*

| # | Title | Author | Year | One-line thesis | 3 most actionable tactics |
|---|-------|--------|------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| 1 | How to Win Friends and Influence People | Dale Carnegie | 1936 | People act on feeling important, not on logic — win them by genuine interest and appreciation. | 1. Remember and use people's names constantly. 2. Ask questions and listen 80/20 instead of pitching. 3. Give honest appreciation before any criticism ("feedback sandwich" done sincerely). |
| 2 | Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion | Robert Cialdini | 1984 | Six universal compliance principles (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity) drive most yeses. | 1. Ask for a small commitment first, then escalate (foot-in-the-door). 2. Show testimonials/user counts at the decision point (social proof). 3. Give something free first to trigger reciprocity. |
| 3 | Pre-Suasion | Robert Cialdini | 2016 | What you do in the moment *before* the ask primes receptivity more than the pitch itself. | 1. Frame attention on a related concept just before the request (e.g., achievement imagery before a sales call). 2. Ask visitors "what's important to you?" upfront, then echo their words. 3. Establish trust signals (shared identity, honest admission) before persuading. |
| 4 | Thinking, Fast and Slow | Daniel Kahneman | 2011 | Two systems run the mind — fast intuitive System 1 and slow effortful System 2 — and System 1's biases predictably distort judgment. | 1. Pre-mortem every big decision ("assume it failed — why?"). 2. Replace gut estimates with base rates (reference-class forecasting). 3. Slow down irreversible decisions; use checklists to engage System 2. |
| 5 | Predictably Irrational | Dan Ariely | 2008 | Humans are irrational in systematic, repeatable ways (anchoring, relativity, zero-price effect) that can be tested and exploited. | 1. Set a high anchor price before revealing the real offer. 2. Add a decoy option to steer choice toward the target tier. 3. Use FREE strategically — zero price creates disproportionate demand. |
| 6 | Nudge | Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein | 2008 | Choice architecture — how options are presented — shapes decisions without restricting freedom. | 1. Make the desired behavior the default (opt-out, not opt-in). 2. Simplify the path: fewer steps, pre-filled forms. 3. Show social-norm feedback ("92% of users do X"). |
| 7 | Misbehaving | Richard Thaler | 2015 | Real "Humans" deviate from textbook "Econs"; mental accounting, fairness, and endowment effects rule actual markets. | 1. Frame prices as gains avoided rather than losses paid. 2. Exploit mental accounting: bundle pains, segregate gains. 3. Use sunk-cost awareness — kill projects by judging future costs only. |
| 8 | The Psychology of Money | Morgan Housel | 2020 | Wealth is a function of behavior and compounding, not IQ — ego, patience, and survival matter most. | 1. Automate saving/investing; let compounding run untouched. 2. Define "enough" in writing to cap lifestyle creep. 3. Keep a margin of safety (cash buffer) so you never sell under pressure. |
| 9 | Poor Charlie's Almanack | Charles T. Munger (ed. Peter Kaufman) | 2005 | Worldly wisdom comes from a "latticework" of mental models across disciplines plus inversion (avoiding stupidity). | 1. Invert: list how a project could fail, then prevent each item. 2. Build a personal checklist of ~30 mental models (incentives, compounding, base rates). 3. "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome" — audit every counterpart's incentives. |
| 10 | Seeking Wisdom | Peter Bevelin | 2007 | Munger-style decision-making: catalog human misjudgment tendencies and counter each with a habit. | 1. Keep a misjudgment journal — log every error against Cialdini/Munger bias list. 2. Demand disconfirming evidence before committing. 3. Decide by expected value with explicit probabilities, not stories. |
| 11 | Fooled by Randomness | Nassim Nicholas Taleb | 2001 | We mistake luck for skill and narrative for cause; randomness dominates outcomes in fat-tailed domains. | 1. Judge decisions by process quality, not outcome. 2. Discount survivorship-biased advice ("follow your passion" from winners). 3. Prefer optionality/barbells over fragile optimization. |
| 12 | The Black Swan | Nassim Nicholas Taleb | 2007 | Rare, high-impact events drive history — yet we systematically underprepare for them while over-predicting the routine. | 1. Keep redundancy (cash, time buffers) for negative Black Swans. 2. Take many small asymmetric bets to catch positive ones. 3. Never expose yourself to ruin — cap downside first. |
| 13 | Thinking in Bets | Annie Duke | 2018 | All decisions are bets under uncertainty — separate decision quality from outcome and think in probabilities. | 1. State every belief as a percentage ("70% confident") and calibrate. 2. Run "resulting" audits: review decisions, never just outcomes. 3. Build a truth-seeking group that rewards dissent. |
| 14 | Superforecasting | Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner | 2015 | The best forecasters are open-minded, numerate updaters who decompose problems and revise in small steps. | 1. Decompose questions into base rate + specifics, then combine. 2. Update forecasts in small increments as news arrives. 3. Keep score of your predictions to calibrate overconfidence. |
| 15 | The Art of Thinking Clearly | Rolf Dobelli | 2013 (EN; 2012 DE orig.) | 99 cognitive errors sabotage clear thought; naming each bias is the first defense. | 1. Kill sunk costs: ask "would I enter this today knowing what I know?" 2. Counter confirmation bias by steelmanning the opposite case. 3. Use "consider the alternative" check before authority-driven choices. |
| 16 | Made to Stick | Chip Heath & Dan Heath | 2007 | Sticky ideas share six traits: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories (SUCCESs). | 1. Open with a broken-schema surprise to grab attention. 2. Translate abstractions into concrete images/numbers. 3. Close every message with a story, not a statistic. |
| 17 | Switch | Chip Heath & Dan Heath | 2010 | Change fails when the emotional Elephant and rational Rider aren't both steered — shape the Path too. | 1. Shrink the change: "just the first 15 minutes." 2. Script the critical moves (exact behaviors, not goals). 3. Tweak the environment so the default is the desired action. |
| 18 | Contagious: Why Things Catch On | Jonah Berger | 2013 | Virality follows six STEPPS triggers: Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories. | 1. Bake social currency in: make sharing make the sharer look good. 2. Attach your message to everyday triggers (Kit Kat + coffee). 3. Engineer high-arousal emotion (awe, amusement) into launches. |
| 19 | Hooked | Nir Eyal | 2014 | Habit-forming products loop through Trigger → Action → Variable Reward → Investment. | 1. Design an external trigger (email/push) that becomes an internal one (boredom → open app). 2. Make the core action completable in <30 seconds. 3. Add variable rewards + stored value (data, followers) to lock users in. |
| 20 | The Power of Habit | Charles Duhigg | 2012 | Habits run on cue-routine-reward loops; keystone habits cascade change through organizations and lives. | 1. Map any habit to cue → routine → reward, then swap only the routine. 2. Install one keystone habit (e.g., daily planning) that drags others along. 3. Use implementation intentions: "When X, I will do Y at Z." |
| 21 | Atomic Habits | James Clear | 2018 | 1% improvements compound; identity-based habits plus environment design beat motivation. | 1. Habit-stack: attach new behavior to an existing one ("After coffee, I will write 2 lines"). 2. Make good habits obvious/easy and bad ones invisible/hard (two-minute rule). 3. Vote for an identity ("I am a writer") with each small win. |
| 22 | Drive | Daniel H. Pink | 2009 | For creative work, autonomy, mastery, and purpose outperform carrots and sticks. | 1. Give teams autonomy over task, time, technique, and team. 2. Replace bonuses with "FedEx days" (ship a passion project). 3. Set Goldilocks goals — just beyond current ability — for flow. |
| 23 | Give and Take | Adam Grant | 2013 | Givers — generous sharers of knowledge, connections, credit — build the networks that win long-term. | 1. Do weekly 5-minute favors (intros, feedback) with no expected return. 2. Run "reciprocity rings": ask the team what they need, crowdsource help. 3. Credit-share publicly; be a "matcher" on accountability, giver on generosity. |
| 24 | Never Split the Difference | Chris Voss & Tahl Raz | 2016 | FBI hostage negotiation tactics — tactical empathy, labeling, calibrated questions — win business deals. | 1. Label emotions ("It seems like you feel…") and pause; let them correct you. 2. Ask calibrated how/what questions ("How am I supposed to do that?") to shift problem-solving burden. 3. Get to "that's right" (not "you're right") before proposing terms; use Ackerman 65/85/95/100 offers. |
| 25 | Getting to Yes | Roger Fisher & William Ury | 1981 | Principled negotiation: separate people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, invent options, use objective criteria. | 1. Ask "why?" until you reach the underlying interest behind each demand. 2. Always know your BATNA — and improve it before negotiating. 3. Propose 3+ options simultaneously to avoid positional haggling. |
| 26 | The Paradox of Choice | Barry Schwartz | 2004 | More options raise anxiety and lower satisfaction — satisficing beats maximizing for happiness. | 1. Cap options at 3–5 in any offer/menu to prevent paralysis. 2. Pre-commit to "good enough" criteria before shopping/hiring. 3. Limit comparison inputs (unsubscribe, fewer reviews) after deciding. |
| 27 | Stumbling on Happiness | Daniel Gilbert | 2006 | We mispredict future feelings due to presentism, impact bias, and the psychological immune system. | 1. Consult "surrogates" — people living the outcome — instead of imagining it. 2. Discount forecasted emotional intensity by ~half (impact bias). 3. When deciding, weight revealed behavior over stated preferences. |
| 28 | Man's Search for Meaning | Viktor E. Frankl | 1946 | Survival and resilience come from meaning — even suffering is bearable with a "why." | 1. Reframe any hardship with "what is this teaching/demanding of me?" 2. Set a concrete future goal (project, person) as an anchor in hard periods. 3. Serve someone else daily — self-transcendence breaks rumination. |
| 29 | Mindset | Carol S. Dweck | 2006 | A growth mindset (abilities are cultivable) versus fixed mindset determines learning, resilience, and achievement. | 1. Praise process/effort ("great strategy"), never talent ("you're smart"). 2. Add "yet" to failures ("I can't do this yet") in team language. 3. Run blameless postmortems focused on systems, not people. |
| 30 | Alchemy | Rory Sutherland | 2019 | Human behavior is psycho-logical, not logical — small irrational tweaks outperform brute-force rational optimization. | 1. Test perception-level changes (packaging, framing, naming) before engineering ones. 2. Solve for the "job" of signaling/status, not just function. 3. Run cheap behavioral experiments (A/B the absurd) — magic beats logic in markets. |

*Sources drawn from: NYT/Amazon bestseller lists, academic citation classics (Kahneman, Thaler, Dweck, Gilbert, Tetlock), Charlie Munger reading lists (Poor Charlie's Almanack, Seeking Wisdom, Influence, Fooled by Randomness), and founder-canon staples (Hooked, Contagious, Atomic Habits, Never Split the Difference, Zero-to-One adjacent behavioral shelf).*
